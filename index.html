About Me

I'm a PhD candidate currently researching on Black Hole X-Ray binary jets and their interactions with the Interstellar Medium 🔭 within the group of Dr. Alexandra Tetarenko at the University of Lethbridge in Alberta, Canada . I have previous research experience on exoplanets 🪐 and Super Massive Black Holes (SMBHs) ⚫️ formation and evolution mechanisms.

I was born in Barcelona, Spain and raised in a small village by the sea called Sant Pere Pescador . 🌊

I received my BSc and MSc at Universitat de Barcelona 📚 and managed Observatori Astronòmic Albanyà 🔭 for a few years before moving to Alberta, Canada 🇨🇦, where I'm pursuing a PhD. Right now I am based in Merate (Italy) through a MITACS Globalink research fellowship.

Also, I am a passionate science communicator and love to teach at all levels. My PhD experience is providing me with some teaching opportunities that give me the chance to exploit that interest.

If you meet me at a conference and want to talk about science or go enjoy a few beers do so at your own risk. I am chatty.

Research Interests

Astrochemistry in Calorimetry

I am working on using the observable properties of the interstellar medium to characterize the energy content deposited by jets at interaction sites. In turn, the retrieval of these properties allows us to make reliable measurements of jet power with a method called calorimetry.

These interaction sites are known to exist within the galactic population of X-ray binaries, and have classically been monitored in the radio band (Dubner et al. 1998, Gallo et al. 2005, Atri et al. 2025, Motta et al. 2025, Mariani et al. 2025).

My particular approach tackles molecular line emission in the sub-mm band to solve the main uncertainties raised by continuum observation methods (Tetarenko et al. 2018, Tetarenko et al. 2020). Find my latest paper here.

The Numerical Simulations View of Jet–ISM Interactions

Jet–ISM interactions are key in the measurement of jet power and the better understanding of jet launching mechanisms.

However, their detailed dynamics are very hard to constrain with imaging only, since the evolution timescales of jet-driven large-scale structures can easily exceed tens of thousands of years.

Luckily, the use of relativistic hydrodynamic simulations bridges the gap between theory and observations, allowing us to get more insight into the intricate dynamics of these regions (e.g., Bosch-Ramon et al. 2011, Gasealahwe et al. 2025, Savard et al. 2025).

Lately, I'm delving into this work myself to bridge the observational behaviour of some BHXB systems to their interaction history.

Observations

My observational work follows a simple philosophy: the wavelength may change, but the scientific question often does not. I use a multiwavelength approach to study how X-ray binaries interact with and inject energy into the interstellar medium, combining radio, submillimetre, and optical observations to build a more complete picture of astrophysical feedback.

So far, I've been fortunate to lead or contribute to successful observing proposals with the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope (JCMT), and the Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope (CFHT) using its SITELLE imaging Fourier transform spectrometer. Although these facilities observe very different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, they all produce the same kind of treasure: spectral cubes—three-dimensional datasets containing a spectrum at every position on the sky.

Treating every pixel as its own spectrum allows us to recover the physical conditions of the gas, its morphology, and its kinematics simultaneously. By measuring these quantities in a spatially resolved way, we can place much stronger constraints on the energy deposited by relativistic jets and significantly reduce the uncertainties associated with understanding X-ray binary feedback into the ISM.

Outreach

I genuinely believe that science belongs to everyone—not just to universities, researchers, or people lucky enough to own a telescope. Throughout the last few years I've tried to make astronomy as accessible as possible, whether that meant speaking to hundreds of people on a stage, guiding families under a dark sky, or helping schools discover why protecting the night matters.

From 2023 to 2024 I had the privilege of serving as President of the Societat Astronòmica de Figueres, where I organized public lectures, community observing nights, and outreach events while promoting awareness of light pollution across the Alt Empordà region.

My biggest international outreach adventure was leading the technical and educational work of the Erasmus+ project Turn Off the Lights, Turn On the Stars. Together with schools and institutions in Spain, Croatia, and Hungary, we developed educational resources that introduced thousands of students to astronomy through the lens of dark-sky conservation.

For five years I also worked as an Outreach Host at Observatori Astronòmic Albanyà, delivering astronomy sessions in Catalan, Spanish, and English to visitors of all ages. At the same time, I served as the observatory's Dark Sky Park Administrator, monitoring sky quality and producing the annual reports required to maintain its International Dark Sky certification.

One of the most memorable moments of my outreach journey was being invited to give a TEDx Manresa talk titled “El temps passa, però què passa amb el temps?”, where I explored the strange nature of time and Special Relativity with an audience of more than 600 people. It reminded me that some of the most beautiful scientific ideas become even more powerful when they're explained with curiosity rather than jargon.

Whether I'm teaching children how to observe the Sun safely, discussing black holes at a conference, or convincing someone to turn off a streetlight, the goal is always the same: making science equitable, welcoming, and genuinely enjoyable for everyone.

Beyond Astronomy

Nature and Mountains

I have always been a mountaineering enthusiast. I was raised close to the Pyrenees, where I developed a love for mountains and spending time outdoors. Since then, I have lived near the Rockies (Canada) and near the Alps (Italy), and each place has given me a new landscape to explore.

I never seem to stop exploring, whether that means hiking through the mountains, cycling across new landscapes, skiing down them, or snowboarding through them. For me, the mountains are humbling, often reminding me that nature is beautiful but unforgiving.

Music

I have been playing musical instruments since I was a child. I have never received any formal musical education and have instead taught myself how to play through years of experimenting, practising and learning by ear (or trying to, anyway).

My main instrument is the guitar, although over the years I have also learned to play bass, piano, violin and some drums. I even tried to teach myself the cello for a while, although bringing a cello with me to Canada was, unfortunately, not really an option.

Photography

I have recently returned to landscape and travel photography. After realising that I seem to travel far more than I take pictures, I decided it was probably time to do something about it. Because yes, the curse of age (even if short by some standards) makes me forget details of where I have been and what I have done.

I am now trying to make photography a more regular part of my travels, with no artistic pretension, just whatever pleases me in the moment.
